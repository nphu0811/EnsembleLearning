They tell you the parts exist, but not what the parts do, and the reader has to imagine the motion the arrows stand for. I wanted Claude to draw <em>the thing itself</em>: a real object whose moving parts behave the way the idea does.</p><p>Anatomy is a skill for Claude Code that does this. You name an idea in one line, Claude invents a machine that does it, and draws it as a live isometric figure you can turn, press and read. Every figure is made of dozens to hundreds of small solid parts, and every number it shows comes from<!-- --> <span class="u">the same model that moves them</span>.</p></div><figure class="live"><div class="live-stage" style="aspect-ratio:1.1111111111111112"></div><figcaption class="live-note"><span class="hint"><span class="hint-item">Drag crown ↑ to wind</span><span class="hint-item"><kbd>S</kbd><span>slow</span></span><span class="hint-item"><kbd>E</kbd><span>apart</span></span></span><span class="live-readout" aria-hidden="true"></span></figcaption></figure><p>This is a mechanical watch movement. It came from one line, <code>/anatomy <!-- -->explain how a mechanical watch keeps time, interactive</code>, and just under two hours of Claude working on its own.<a class="ref" href="#note-1" id="ref-1" aria-label="Note 1">1</a><span class="side" role="note"><span aria-hidden="true">1</span>One hour and fifty-four minutes of active time, built in one pass and checked by the same agent.</span></p><p>Drag the crown upward to wind it: the ratchet turns, the click ticks and the power reserve climbs. Press <kbd>S</kbd> to slow time to a twentieth, and again to a hundredth, and you can watch the pallet fork let the fifteen-tooth escape wheel through <strong>half a tooth at a time</strong>. Press<!-- --> <kbd>E</kbd> to lift the bridges apart and see the train underneath, its wheels cut to true ratios: the fourth wheel turns once a minute and the centre wheel once an hour.</p><section id="objects" class="section" data-section=""><div class="section-head"><h2>Objects, not diagrams</h2><span class="section-index" aria-hidden="true">01</span></div><p>The skill rests on one rule, written at the top of its instructions, above everything else:</p><blockquote class="pull">The object is the explanation.<cite>SKILL.md, “The one idea that matters”</cite></blockquote><p>No boxes, no arrows, no labels floating in space. Claude has to invent a real, well-made thing whose working <em>is</em> the concept, and say in one sentence what moves and what that motion means.</p><p>A figure fails in two ways, and I watched it fail both ways many times before the rule existed. <strong>Too abstract:</strong> stacked plates, floating slabs and labelled boxes that don’t read as anything. <strong>Too flat:</strong> one big slab with a few lines on it, with no volume, no parts and nothing to discover. The cure for both is the same, a recognisable machine built from many small parts that each do something.<a class="ref" href="#note-2" id="ref-2" aria-label="Note 2">2</a><span class="side" role="note"><span aria-hidden="true">2</span>The machine is never copied from a reference picture. A reference can give the finish, the materials and the mood; the object is invented.</span></p><p>Some ideas map almost by themselves. A queue is a marble run: two hoppers feed one sloping lane, and the marbles wait in order behind an escapement until a timer drum takes them. A cache that forgets its oldest entry is a bookcase with four places on top, most recently used at the left. Others take more invention. They are what make it read as a solid, made thing rather than a sketch.</p><dl class="rules"><dt>One camera</dt><dd>Isometric, looking down at 30° from a corner. Nothing in perspective, so parallel edges stay parallel.</dd><dt>Solids</dt><dd>Every part is filled and painted in order, so what is in front hides what is behind. No see-through edges.</dd><dt>Four tones</dt><dd>Each side is shaded by the way it faces the light, with a lighter top.</dd><dt>One accent</dt><dd>Exactly one part is lit: the one the reader should watch.</dd><dt>Lines resolve</dt><dd>Every line ends on a face, another line or a dot. No leader lines, balloons or arrows.</dd><dt>Detail at 4×</dt><dd>At normal size it reads as one machine. Zoomed in, every region still has something true to find.</dd><dt>No words inside</dt><dd>Text lives around the drawing, never on it, apart from one tiny engraved label at most.</dd><dt>True numbers</dt><dd>Every readout comes from the model that moves the parts.</dd></dl><p>A figure is also <em>a small program</em>. The fixes that mattered most came from watching a figure play and describing what felt off.</p><p>It is slow when it is good. The skill asks for at least forty parts in every figure and will not trade detail for speed on its own. If you need something quick, say so, and expect a Figure rather than a Hero.</p><p>3D is a beta. It turns one subject at a time, and a full audit of a turning figure takes fifteen to twenty minutes, so it is worth asking for only when a part really has to move in depth.</p><p>References are for mood. Give it a photograph and it will take the finish, not the object. A faithful copy of someone else’s drawing is not what it is for.</p><p>So treat the first figure as <span class="u">a draft</span>.
